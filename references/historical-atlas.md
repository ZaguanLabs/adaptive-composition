# Historical atlas: problems that recur

## Scope and evidence

The center of this survey is approximately 1966–2026, with a few explicitly older precursors. It follows graphic and information design alongside industrial design, terminals, and HCI. It is a selective, source-grounded working library, not an exhaustive global history or proof that old practice always worked.

Dates describe original work where established; a source's upload date or modern reissue is not the original date. The **transfer** paragraphs below are this skill's interpretation. A publisher description establishes a book's subject and provenance, not that its full text has been reviewed. See [sources](sources.md) for evidence types and limitations.

## 1. Series and programmes: 1961–1980s

**Evidence.** Romek Marber's Penguin work (1961 onward, an older precursor) organizes shared cover elements while admitting different illustrations. Karl Gerstner's *Designing Programmes* (1964, another precursor) develops systematic approaches to generating design. Josef Müller-Brockmann's *Grid Systems in Graphic Design* (1981) addresses organizing two- and three-dimensional applications [S01–S03].

**Original problem.** Make many related artifacts coherent and producible without solving every decision from scratch.

**Transfer.** Separate semantic roles, reusable rules, and controlled variation. A publication family suggests component variants and shared typography, not identical page templates. Define why a region exists before fixing its geometry.

**Limit.** Paper grids do not specify source order, focus behavior, variable content, or runtime transitions. Mathematical regularity alone cannot establish a readable or useful interface.

## 2. Identity at many sizes and on many objects: 1970s onward

**Evidence.** Paul Rand's IBM eight-bar identity dates to 1972 in IBM's records; current asset guidance explicitly includes optical compensation between black and white stripes. NASA's 1970s graphics programme applies an identity across publications, signs, and vehicles [S04, S30].

**Original problem.** Maintain recognition through different production processes, scales, backgrounds, and physical uses.

**Transfer.** Specify an identity family and optical variants where needed. Review a mark at actual rendered size; vector scalability does not guarantee perceptual scalability. A recognizable compact treatment may require a different lockup or less detail.

**Limit.** Follow the actual brand's approved assets; a historical example does not authorize altering someone else's logo. Brand manuals document practice, not comparative usability results.

## 3. Wayfinding as a coordinated system: 1957–1979 and beyond

**Evidence.** Kinneir and Calvert's British road-sign work spans the period leading into the 1960s. Mexico 1968 and Munich 1972 demonstrate coordinated event identities and signs. The AIGA/DOT symbol work was developed in stages in 1974 and 1979; a government research report discusses both design criteria and comprehension evaluation [S05–S07].

**Original problem.** Help people recognize categories, locate themselves, and make decisions across a large environment under limited attention and varied language backgrounds.

**Transfer.** Navigation needs consistent destinations, local context, decision-point cues, and comprehensible symbols. A repeated icon family can connect views, but labels and location information still matter. Distinguish “where I am” from “where this action takes me.”

**Limit.** Claims of universal visual language must be tested. Cultural meaning, familiarity, language, disability, and the consequences of a mistake change the requirements. Credit design teams rather than reducing collective programmes to a single celebrated name.

## 4. Flexible systems for diverse content: Unigrid, 1977

**Evidence.** The NPS account credits publications chief Vincent Gleason's initiative and Massimo Vignelli's design contribution. Unigrid standardizes format and production while allowing varied arrangements of images, text, and maps [S08].

**Original problem.** Produce a recognizable, manageable publication system for very different parks and stories.

**Transfer.** A design system should coordinate content types and relationships while allowing each page's main evidence to shape the composition. Shared rules can enable variation instead of suppressing it.

**Limit.** Folding and print production impose finite physical formats. A web component must also handle continuous resizing, text expansion, input, and state. A fold is an analogy for sequencing, not an implementation recipe for an accordion.

## 5. Maps and quantitative evidence: 1967–1990s

**Evidence.** Jacques Bertin's *Semiology of Graphics* first appeared in French in 1967. Edward Tufte's *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information* appeared in 1983. Ben Shneiderman's 1996 *The Eyes Have It* connects information types to exploration tasks [S09, S11, S17].

**Original problem.** Make relationships, variation, and large bodies of evidence inspectable.

**Transfer.** Select visual encodings for the comparison being made. Preserve scale and context when reducing a chart; use overview and accessible details where that supports exploration. A dense table can be the correct composition for side-by-side decisions.

**Limit.** Do not convert observations into unlabeled summaries or treat minimal ink as an absolute objective. Interactive filtering changes the visible evidence; expose active filters, missing data, and time context.

## 6. Interactive structure: 1968 onward

**Evidence.** Engelbart and his team's 1968 demonstration includes structured document navigation, editing, and collaboration. It provides direct historical evidence that dynamic views and interaction were already substantial design concerns [S31].

**Original problem.** Help people manipulate and navigate complex information using an interactive system.

**Transfer.** The representation can change while the underlying document and task persist. A compressed overview and a focused working view need a reliable connection.

**Limit.** A compelling demonstration is not evidence that its training demands suit all audiences. Do not romanticize unfamiliar command systems or claim an unbroken causal chain to a particular modern component.

## 7. Terminal and command composition: 1970s onward

**Evidence.** Ritchie and Thompson's UNIX paper describes command interfaces, shared I/O conventions, redirection, and pipes. The available Bell Labs copy is the expanded 1978 version of work published in 1974 [S20].

**Original problem.** Make tools work together through predictable representations and interfaces in constrained computing environments.

**Transfer.** Keep an interface's semantic operations independent from its full-screen presentation. Where in scope, provide usable textual output and scripting routes alongside an interactive view. Predictable commands and stable records can carry identity and structure without decorative graphics.

**Limit.** A command line and a full-screen TUI are different interfaces. UNIX pipe composition is not visual composition, and historical terse output is not automatically good guidance for novices. Modern cell widths and terminal accessibility require separate engineering [S32–S34].

## 8. Industrial controls and early GUI systems: 1970s–1980s

**Evidence.** Rams's principles connect product form with use and intelligibility. The Xerox Star designers' 1982 article describes consistent commands, conceptual models, visible objects, and design tradeoffs. Susan Kare's 1982–1983 graph-paper icon sketches document deliberate work at individual-pixel scale [S10, S13, S35].

**Original problem.** Make unfamiliar machines intelligible and operable under limited display and input conditions.

**Transfer.** Compose controls around understandable operations and visible state. At small sizes, select recognizable detail rather than shrinking indiscriminately. Support learning that carries from one operation to another.

**Limit.** A desktop metaphor can become misleading when copied literally. A historical icon is neither automatically familiar today nor inherently international. Native conventions may differ between platforms.

## 9. Human factors and action: 1980s–2000s

**Evidence.** Shneiderman dates his original golden rules to 1985; the online text is a later revision. Norman's 1988 book and later essays distinguish action possibilities, learned conventions, and signifiers. MacKenzie, Sellen, and Buxton's 1991 study examines pointing and dragging with different devices [S12, S14, S19].

**Original problem.** Reduce errors and effort and make operation comprehensible, learnable, and recoverable.

**Transfer.** Include feedback, current mode, control discoverability, task completion, and reversal in design specifications. Evaluate input effort with the actual device and task.

**Limit.** Guidelines need contextual interpretation. A movement model is not a general law of human preference; a remembered chunk count is not a menu-length limit [S38]. Historical interface guidelines can contain advice that conflicts with present accessibility or task requirements.

## 10. Music, editorial expression, and digital experimentation: 1970s–1990s

**Evidence.** Music-design collections connect artwork, posters, devices, and listening culture. Punk graphics and Emigre's typographic debates expose different purposes for visual expression. Cooper and her collaborators' *Information Landscapes* (1994) explores dynamic digital information beyond the fixed page [S15, S16, S28, S29].

**Original problem.** Communicate cultural identity, attract attention, invite interpretation, and explore new media rather than merely present neutral information.

**Transfer.** Make deliberate distinctions between expressive surfaces and operational controls. A distinctive music interface can preserve an album's atmosphere while keeping playback, prices, and navigation clear. Digital typography can organize changing information instead of imitating a printed page.

**Limit.** Challenging artwork is not an excuse for unreadable instructions. Experimental prototypes are evidence of exploration, not proof of superiority. The LP/cassette example remains a design exercise here: no specific release has been established as a verified before/after case study.

## 11. Universal design: 1997 and its continuing application

**Evidence.** The Center for Universal Design's 1997 principles address equitable use, flexibility, perceptibility, effort, and tolerance for error across environments and products [S21].

**Original problem.** Design for a broader range of people instead of treating difference as an afterthought.

**Transfer.** Include alternative input and perception paths in the initial composition. The quality of a system includes access to its work, not only the elegance of its typical view.

**Limit.** Broad principles are not a compliance test. Modern web standards provide more specific requirements, and terminal/native accessibility need their own platform checks [S22–S24].

## 12. Web adaptation and modern implementation: 2010–2026

**Evidence.** Marcotte's 2010 responsive design article already goes beyond simple fluid scaling, including changed arrangements. Current HTML image guidance distinguishes resolution selection from art direction; CSS container queries support local layout decisions. GNOME's guidance treats window-size adaptation as preserving functionality [S18, S25, S26, S36].

**Original problem.** Make one evolving experience work across unpredictable available space and capabilities.

**Transfer.** Combine intrinsic layout, meaningful composition changes, semantic structure, and state continuity. Use modern mechanisms to express an older concern with appropriate form.

**Limit.** A handful of fixed device mockups does not cover a continuum of sizes, input combinations, and user settings. Neither “mobile first” nor “desktop first” substitutes for actual content and task analysis.

## Extending this atlas

This selection is strongest in European and North American published design/HCI, with selected Mexican and Japanese examples. It is not representative of every geography, language, craft tradition, community, or form of disability advocacy. For a culturally specific brief, research practitioners and sources from that context and the target writing system; do not merely apply this canon and add local motifs.

Treat this atlas as a fallback index, not the starting bibliography for culturally situated work. Begin with the product's actual languages, institutions, tools, vernacular forms, disability communities, and living practitioners. Seek accounts from creators and affected communities alongside institutional collections; notice who preserved, translated, credited, or omitted the work. A translated museum label and a contemporary practitioner's account establish different things.

Add precedents only when they change a decision. Record:

- creator or community, audience, place, language, date, and production conditions;
- what the source directly establishes and whose account it represents;
- the specific constraint and transferable relationship, not a detachable motif;
- a counterexample or reason the transfer may fail now;
- the present task check that will decide whether to keep the idea.

Distinguish research read in full from catalog descriptions, original sources from later interpretations, and observed effects from proposed applications. Do not equate a famous surviving artifact with a representative sample of historical practice, and do not add one token example to imply global coverage.

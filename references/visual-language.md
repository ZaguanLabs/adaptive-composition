# Creating a visual language that can adapt

Use this when a brief has no established identity, or when the existing interface is so generic that preserving it would preserve little. This is not a request to invent a new brand for every responsive task. If the project already has a useful visual system, work within it.

## Begin with a position, not a style menu

Extract two or three consequential tensions from the brief, content, and audience. Examples include archival versus live, exact versus expressive, civic versus intimate, or expert density versus public legibility. These must describe the product's real purpose, not fashionable mood-board adjectives.

Collect evidence native to the subject: actual content, tools, materials, language, imagery, diagrams, environments, and relevant cultural references. For work grounded in a place or community, research its practitioners and conventions directly. Do not manufacture “local character” from stereotypes or borrow protected artwork.

Write a short design position:

> This interface should feel **[quality tied to purpose]** because **[product truth]**, while remaining **[operational requirement]**. It should not become **[specific anti-goal]**.

The position is a decision filter, not user evidence.

## Establish a signature relationship

Distinctive systems usually have a characteristic relationship, not just a pile of unusual tokens. Choose one primary compositional gesture and a quieter counterpoint. Examples include:

- A precise information rail crossing a field of expressive imagery.
- Oversized editorial titles paired with compact, aligned operational data.
- A continuous timeline interrupted only by exceptional states.
- Dense tabular evidence framed by a restrained color field and one recognizable mark.

The gesture must work with real content and remain identifiable when the composition changes. Do not make every region exceptional. Contrast needs a stable layer to act against.

## Define a bounded grammar

Specify choices as relationships and valid ranges rather than one screenshot.

| Layer | Decide | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Typography | Roles, contrast between roles, measure, casing, numeric treatment, fallback behavior, writing-system needs | Selecting a typeface before knowing what the text must do |
| Color | Semantic roles, expressive field, contrast pairs, non-color redundancy, light/dark or material response | A large palette with no responsibility for each color |
| Shape and line | Edge character, enclosure, dividers, radii, icon treatment, data marks | Applying the same container treatment to every fragment |
| Space and grid | Rhythm, alignment rules, deliberate breaks, density, reading measure | A compulsory column count or spacing scale detached from content |
| Image | Whole object, focal crop, documentary evidence, texture, or generated material; fallback and attribution | Treating every image as a cover crop or decorative hero |
| Motion and sound | What change they explain, timing character, reduced/quiet alternatives | Ambient effects that compete with operation or carry essential meaning alone |
| Language | Naming, sentence rhythm, label directness, specialist terminology | A visual identity whose voice becomes generic product copy |

One or two layers may carry most of the expression. Let operational text and controls stay dependable. A distinctive interface need not make every button, label, and error state novel.

## Derive variants instead of shrinking the original

For each supported condition, decide where the signature relationship moves:

- Which cue carries recognition when imagery is absent, monochrome, spoken, or very small?
- Which typographic role, spatial rhythm, phrase, sound, or mark can assume more identity work?
- Which expressive material may crop, simplify, sequence, or disappear because another cue preserves recognition?
- Which operational layer must remain conventional for the platform and task?

Create at least one constrained and one spacious composition with the same representative content. Add a nonvisual or low-fidelity expression only when that surface is in scope. Preserve family resemblance through roles and relationships, not identical coordinates.

## Challenge generic output

Use these checks as diagnostics, not numeric scores:

- **Swap test:** could the same composition, type treatment, gradient, illustration, and copy fit an unrelated product with only a logo change? If yes, the system has not yet expressed a product truth.
- **No-logo test:** do the signature relationship, language, and content treatment still distinguish the experience?
- **Reduction test:** in grayscale, low resolution, a narrow pane, or text-only output, which cues remain? If none, identity depends on one fragile effect.
- **Neighbor test:** can people distinguish related objects or states within the system, not merely recognize the brand shell?
- **Content stress test:** does the visual language survive long names, missing images, errors, live updates, and localized text without becoming anonymous or chaotic?
- **Restraint test:** is the unusual treatment concentrated where it serves invitation, recognition, or meaning, with ordinary operation still clear?

Recognition and preference require evidence from the intended audience. An agent can identify likely sameness, inconsistency, or loss of cues; it cannot certify that an identity is memorable or culturally appropriate.

## Deliver a usable identity note

For substantial new design work, record:

1. The design position and anti-goals.
2. The signature relationship and its purpose.
3. The small formal grammar: roles, ranges, and dependencies.
4. How recognition migrates across constrained, spacious, nonvisual, or degraded conditions in scope.
5. Operational areas that deliberately follow platform convention.
6. Open cultural, licensing, production, or audience questions and how to test them.

This note should be enough to make the next screen belong to the same family without cloning the first one.
